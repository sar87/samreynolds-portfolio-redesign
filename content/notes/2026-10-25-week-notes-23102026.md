### Conservation Evidence Copilot v2

I haven't properly considered the versioning of the Conservation Evidence Copilot (CEC), but I probably should. This week I have been making both functional tweaks and quality of life updates to the CEC. These include:

- Introducing the PDF report download function, which also captures the models decision making processes that led to the answer.
- Linking the Actions on the results page directly to their action page on the main Conservation Evidence (CE) website.
- Improving the faithfulness of the summaries provided to the original CE summary. I found that in my original efforts to reduce context bloat, this had an accidental side effect of the final summary agent only receiving the first 100 characters of the relevant studies. So some of the results presented weren't set in the right context. This has now changed, it sees everything. This means that the Action summaries are now broken down by the outcome that was measured, whether it was Abundance, Species Richness, Survival or something else. Much cleaner. 
- To cut down the time from question to answer I have also been doing some optimising. The summary agent now runs Sonnet 5.5, rather than the older Opus 4.6. It also now generates each action summary in parallel, rather than doing each section sequentially. This has significantly sped the process up. 
- It is now, which will be much to Bill's delight, been optimised for mobile phone users!
- Finally, each query used to spin up an 800MB embedding model, so 10 concurrent queries would mean the same model would be taking up 8 GB of memory. Not ideal. Now, the embedding model is shared by all queries! The server will thank me (Claude).

This version of the app is now live on our server and is ready for some more user testing with out Evidence Champions network.

### Synthesaurus v2

For many moons now I have been travelling around talking to people about the work we did on Synthesaurus v1, which is the in-house name for the AI pipeline which ingests normalised papers from our downloader (in .TEI format) and then bounces them between various models and processes to be able to identify the ones you need for your review. When we built this pipeline, we were self hosting a 70B quantised version of Deepseek across multiple machines. We now can use much better open models, and we have much better hardware, time for a redeisgn. We are building a wholly new version of the pipeline, and have projects set up with other academics within Cambridge to work on the trick aspects such as extracting data from graphs. We are now moving faster so that we can make the most of the access we have to academic resources. Watch this space.

### Conservation Evidence MCP

MCP stands for Model Context Protocol, and it should (maybe) allow for a new way of interacting with the CE project as a whole. 

I imagine this mostly as a massive filing cabinet and next to it, a stack of encyclopaedias. The filing cabinet contains draws, and each draw holds multiple folders, within each folder is information in various forms (documents, spreadsheets, slide decks etc.). Each draw and each folder has a sticky note on it, that has a page number. The page number refers to a page in the encyclopaedia. Going to that page tells you how to interact and interpret the information within the draw. 

The idea is that people can use their own AI agents, send them to the MCP and, using the organised information and the book of skills, the agent can get the context it needs to be able to work with the information held inside. 

This should allow people to ask broader questions about the CE project, as well as information about evidence held for certain species, or evidence from certain geographic regions. We can also, along side the filing cabinet and the stack of encyclopaedias add a tool box. One of the tools an agent could tap into could be the Conservation Evidence Copilot. 

This is very much work in progress, but I think should make for a fun side project. 

### Talking about  a career in conservation

I was invited alongside Catherine Sayer (IUCN), Hollie Booth (Darwin-Hamied Fellow) and Jakob Poffley (Geography PhD student) to talk to undergraduates who are looking at potentially pursuing a career in conservation. As always, there is a healthy dose of survivor bias on the panel. Asking us what works is, as once said by the legend [Bo Burnham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bo_Burnham), is a bit like asking advice from a lottery winner - "liquidise your assets, buy lottery tickets, it works!".  

In some ways I feel like an imposter to give advice on 'what it takes' to have a career in conservation. It is not something that I necessarily aimed to do, and I always thought that I definitely wouldn't want an academic career. However, I am drawn to the creative freedom that you have in academia and the openness to explore interesting questions with other curious people. 

Some of my points to the students were:

- Don't underestimate luck and timing in the making of a career. However, there are things you can do to maximise your exposure to luck and timing. 
- Build a network and put yourself out there. 
- Don't be afraid to move sideways, it leads to exposure to new ideas and ways of working, you can always build a path back to where you want to be later. Even if you feel like you know exactly what you want - it might change in ways you don't expect. 
- Success in conservation can often be built on privilege. Those who can afford to take the unpaid internship. Those with family connections. Those with the money, or the financial security to take on a loan to do a masters. The longer you are able to keep taking shots, the more likely you are to succeed. Not everyone can do this. Some people have to take a job that can support them. I had it easier than most, but still worked in pubs and shops for years while I did unpaid internships, did a masters degree and fired off application after application that went without reply. It's not easy. 
- Be curious - conservation is not just NGOs and academia. A lot of what happens on the ground is decided by everyone else, such as civil service and private organisations (For example the Crown Estate, the National Trust, Forestry England). And often, these are the groups that make the biggest impact. These are the groups NGOs and Academia are often trying to influence - so why not be on the inside? A person who cares about biodiversity in a room of people who don't can be seen as a negative, but it can also mean that you can have an outsized impact if you can convince them it matters. 
- Rejection is coming. As undergraduates at Cambridge you are unlikely to have experienced much rejection. However, life stops being on rails once the sun sets on graduation day. No one is telling you what to do anymore, you have to decide. 
- Don't compare your progress to others. You can't worry about other people's luck, privilege, timing or skills.
