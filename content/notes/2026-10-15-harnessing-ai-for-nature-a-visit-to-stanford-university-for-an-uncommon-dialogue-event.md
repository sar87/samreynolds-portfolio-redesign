## Stanford University - Uncommon Dialogue

*These are some top of the head notes and reflections on the Uncommon Dialogue event I attended. As with all these notes, I type from my mind onto the page with only basic levels of organisation and proofreading. This is all to say - apologies if I ramble and/or miss important details/nuances ...*

![](</images/notes/Image (2).jpeg>)

This week I was invited to attend an event at Stanford University on harnessing AI for Nature. It was a fantastic group of people from a range of tech organisations (i.e., google and anthropic), NGOs (i.e., WWF and TNC), policy (i.e., Environmental Policy Innovation Centre, Nature Capital Alliance) and Academia (i.e., MIT, Stanford, UCSD). Here I have collated some of my reflections from the discussions that we had on the day. I have omitted attributions to any particular statements, as the meeting was undertaken under Chatham House rules. 

The general framing and overarching question for the event was "What can we do together that we cannot do apart? and, what decisions to we need to make to work more together?" - and the TL;DR is 'What-if machines'.

![](</images/notes/Image (1).jpeg>)

 We started with some updates from the perspectives of AI for Nature from three different stakeholders in the space, Conservation NGOs, Academia and the Technology sector. 

## Updates from different sectors

I have captured some of the general themes from these updates as bullet points.

### Conservation NGOs

- NGO data is specific to the project, place and researchers who collected it. The struggle is balancing niche, local usefulness against the broader AI conversation. 
- Lots of hope pinned on AI for decision support and efficiency.
- AI could shorten the distance from ambition to impact. 
- Two provocations: what if we could test interventions before doing them, and what if we could talk to maps and models? 
- There's a lot we don't understand about how models work, so literacy and fluency in AI is a critical condition for responsible use. There are [some existing case studies](https://learn.mcgovern.org/Building-Organizational-Readiness-1cf70021ed8c80389095d3dd998568e4) of how we can make the conservation sector more 'AI ready'.
- There are many signals in the data that we don't yet know how to make work for us.

### Academia

- AI in ecology isn't a monolith and isn't only large foundation models. It's used for curating ecological observations, representing ecosystems, handling documents, and writing code. AI is used in conservation but not by everyone; most people still look at data by hand. Everyone is using it to write code, though.
- More capable models don't mean more consistent models. Models are universally bad at rare things, new things, distinguishing similar things, and staying consistent when conditions change. Predictions are shaped by the distribution of training data. AI is essentially a detection process conditioned on unknown covariates.
- Evaluation is rapidly becoming the most important component of AI, and verifiability is hard in ecology. Who is checking what, and for what? Not everything is verifiable from existing data, verifiers need the right skill set, and AI systems are imperfect observers.
- Resource trade-offs matter: the value versus the cost of information, centralising versus distributing effort, and what is "good enough" for the decisions we actually need to make.
- We don't understand the oceans. Tools in play include FathomNet for imagery, acoustic monitoring, and machine learning reshaping species distribution models for ocean management such as fishing and shipping routes. Global Fishing Watch can track fishing activity at ocean-basin scale.
- What's next for marine AI: multimodal signal integration, turning information into ecological understanding beyond classification, and swarms of autonomous vehicles exploring the deep sea. Ecology has been hypothesis-driven, but with this much data the pattern comes first and the meaning is inferred afterwards. AI lowers the barrier between people and data.
- Cautions: dual use of these technologies, for instance using models to find the last fish stocks. There's a mismatch between where models and data are developed and where biodiversity actually is. How do we give agency to people in biodiverse regions? Classifying the unknown is a real problem: marine eDNA is mostly unresolved taxonomically because the reference databases are unresolved.

### Technology Sector

- Earth AI is a family of foundation models coupled with an agent. AlphaEarth is mostly about reducing friction. 
- There are cases where embeddings aren't enough. Examples: an inventory of small woody features at 30cm resolution with partners in Oxford, which found they account for 75% of national forest stock; distribution models for 104 species at 80m resolution (not yet released); and pan-tropical deforestation work.
- How do we get more data? The planet AI pipeline claims to run from AI to data to actionable insight: a global archive, an agent, derived layers, queryable embeddings. Agents need context in the form of relevant data, local knowledge and expert guidance, and they need goals to aim for. 
- Open questions: how to do reliable summarisation with LLMs and tie it to decision-making, and how to make the most of the new sensors and satellites available. Mentioned an Amazon project called [Amazon.ia](https://www.amazoniainteligenciaambiental.org/en-us/)

### Short QnA

- On AI literacy and fluency: There's lots of demand from non-science teams in NGOs. People want a guide, not a guidebook. We discussed the idea of building a conservation AI literacy tool, training people on it, and having them deliver training onward.
- There was a general reflection on the fact that it's not always a big model or big tool for every problem. We need more design thinking about how we use AI.
- We also discussed whether AI uptake slower in conservation than other fields. It was pointed out that there's a much clearer cost-to-value trade-off in processing camera trap data than in producing yet another map that can't be fully evaluated against the last one. Distributed open-source systems actually need more technical know-how, not less. For evaluation, traceability and credit, there needs to be private evaluation data held back for assessment.

## The takeaways

From the meeting itself the main idea that the broader group coalesced around was 'What-if machines'. Tools that allow us to ask specific ecological and conservation questions that draw from our current ecological understanding to predict the effectiveness of conservation interventions. This raises other questions such as, can we have a data commons that incorporates ecological data alongside social data? How do we ensure the security of this data to avoid tampering, especially if this data is used to calculate biodiversity metrics that feed into biodiversity markets? 

The main ask from the room to funders, tech organisations and CEOs of conservation organisations is how do we secure the funding that allows us to build the un-sexy infrastructure that we need to be able to facilitate this future. Building flashy tools is all well and good, but they need to be underpinned by interoperable data, with the gaps and limitations of the data clearly defined. What we need is some kind of endowment that allows for the development of this data commons, with the ongoing funds that will be required to update and maintain it. 

We need structures for benefit sharing, so that people who feed into the data commons can benefit from their data being used to guide decision making. 

There is also of course the risk that the questions that we are asking are not the right ones. As famously characterised by Douglas Adams, the answer to life the universe and everything is 42. If we don't ask the right questions, we are going to have non-sensical answers from which to operate on. 

As always with these types of meetings, it takes a little while for everyone to start speaking the same language. Within AI there is a lot of vocabulary which has general and specific meanings which overlaps. For example, what is a tool? Is a tool specific to tool use by agents, such as access to a calculator or certain predefined functions, or is a tool an AI application such as the Conservation Evidence Copilot? Both are logical extensions of the word tool, but leads to unaligned conversations. 

Which leads nicely onto alignment - a big area of discussion in AI right now as we race towards better and better models as the window on our ability to ensure they are setting out on the 'right' path closes. In the context of conservation, what does alignment look like? What optimal biodiversity looks like for one group of people will be different for another. We will likely need some kind of pluralistic approach where where they are multiple potential alignments depending on the context you are working in. 

None of these are simple questions with simple solutions, and it is nearly impossible to have all the right people in the room to be able to cover off these topics in a satisfactory manner. Also, the technology is progressing so quickly that these essential conversations and considerations are not happening quickly enough to guide current development. 

However, that doesn't mean we should not have these conversations, and I am glad that this Uncommon Dialogue allowed for new connections between people working in this space. I am very grateful to have been involved.
