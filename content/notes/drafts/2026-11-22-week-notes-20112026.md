This week began with working on preparing datasets for Synthesaurus, and ended with a trip to Heidelberg. 

### Synthesaurus - preparing the data

I have been breaking down a large snapshot of XML papers that have been provided by Taposaur, our agentic paper downloader. The aim of which is to be able to recreate the conditions use to create the butterfly and moth synopsis as manually curated by the Conservation Evidence team. The full snapshot contained somewhere in the region of 600,000 XMLs, narrowing those down to ones containing all the metadata needed for Synthesaurus (namely title, abstract and full text) narrowed the dataset down to approximately 300,000 files. 

I have then matched these up to, firstly the accepted set of papers in the synopsis and then also the papers that were reviewed by the team but rejected. This leaves a set of ~150,000 XML files that can be used to test the pipeline. The hope is that this week I can run a few smoke tests and get this evaluation underway. The reason I couldn't do it this week was because of my trip to Germany.

### Springer-Nature European Research Advisory Council - Heidelberg

On Wednesday I flew out to Heidelberg for a two day (or rather two half day) meeting for the Springer-Nature European Research Advisory Council (ERAC) meeting. This meeting happens once a year, and you serve on the Council for a two-year term. So this was my second and final meeting. The theme of this years meeting was:

> **From Lab to Society: Optimising economic and societal impact from Europe’s investment in research**

In the lead up to the meeting they asked us to consider two questions: 

1. How is your organisation currently approaching tech transfer and innovation?
2. What emerging issues and challenges have you had to face?

To think about this I was first directed by [Anil](https://anil.recoil.org/) to [Ross Anderson](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/archive/rja14/)'s "[Cambridge University – the Unauthorised History](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/archive/rja14/unauthorised.html)". The central aim of this essay is to analyse why Cambridge, throughout its history, has been a place of innovation in both the sciences and the arts. Ross attributes two things as central to Cambridge's success, institutional self-government and academic freedom. That these two elements have created an environment of creative destruction and the efficient destruction of error. Ideas are two-a-penny, and it is the efficient destruction of error that leads to the truth.  Cambridge to act like a flamethrower setting 'cultural forest fires' for hundreds of years, burning away the bad ideas, leaving the resilient ones, and creating new fertile space for new ideas to take hold. This take on innovation was slightly counter to the feeling around the table at ERAC, where academic freedom seemed to be viewed more as a barrier to innovation and, perhaps more accurately, commercialisation. Seen as an argument that is used by academics to shake of the yolk of any type of governance that may be seen as restrictive. 

I also spoke to [Emma](https://www.enterprise.cam.ac.uk/team/emma-salgard-cunha/) at [Cambridge Enterprise](https://www.enterprise.cam.ac.uk/), to get more of a practical take on Cambridge's approach to commercialisation and tech-transfer: 

> We have a really mature commercialisation infrastructure covering all disciplines of the university. There are specialised functions to enable consultancy and contract research, traditional technology transfer, social innovation and spinout and we also have an internal venture capital fund as well as in-house proof of concept to help de-risk projects on their way to market. This is helped by a very active investment ecosystem in Cambridge especially around Life Sciences and Deeptech. It is also helped by a set of established (more than 20 years) founder-friendly policies around IP ownership and commercialisation which strongly incentivise researchers and students to engage in these activities - for example, non-registerable IP is owned by the researchers, they receive up to 90% of commercial revenue from licensing directly, the institution has low caps on the amount of equity it takes in spinouts, and researchers can also choose whether to commercialise IP including patents within or outside of the institutional support.

In terms of blockers: 

> The big story in commercialisation is around the move to software and data commercialisation, which requires a change in thinking around intellectual property and commercialisation strategies. Timelines can be shorter, due diligence can be more complex, and there is also often a closer relationship between the output of research and the actual asset which is commercialised - which means that publication/open access distribution of the entire asset has to be factored into our commercial plans.

I also spoke from my own experience at Cambridge with regards to commercialisation and the exposure we are given to entrepreneurship. I spoke about my experience with i-Teams in the early days of the Conservation Evidence Copilot; the ai@CAM sprints programme, which gave me experience of developing this tool and taking part in product pitches; and the Enterprise Tech programme that is run by the Judge Business School. 

It seemed to me that Cambridge had a relatively mature outlook on entrepreneurship compared to many of the other European institutions at the meeting. One thing I did not appreciate was how European universities are looking at research commercialisation as a mechanism for bringing more money into the University sector.

European universities do not charge student fees, and so maximising student attendance does not improve their bottom line. The majority of the money that supports the universities are the overheads from large grants. They said that the majority of their staff have become 'Grantreprenerurs' - expert in writing the applications needed to bring money into the institution. However, there are too many universities, and too few grants. So European Universities need a different way to guarantee their future existence.  

Springer-Nature have smelled an opportunity. They see themselves as uniquely placed to join up venture capitalists and potential investors with researchers. Using what they call their 'privileged access' to cutting edge research, they plan to build mechanisms through which VCs can pay to access research that Springer-Nature deem fit for commercialisation.  There were many unanswered questions, do researchers opt in? Is this only looking at the Springer-Nature portfolio, or is is all open access papers as well? Is Springer-Nature best placed to recognise what research has commercialisation potential? All interesting questions.
